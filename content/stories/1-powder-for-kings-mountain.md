[[Mary McKeehan (1751)|Mary McKeehan]] was born in England on 15 December 1751, and her family crossed to Pennsylvania in the late 1760s. She learned to make gunpowder as a girl, perhaps from her father, who is thought to have been named [[David McKeehan]]. [[John Patton (1749)|John Patton]], a Scotch-Irish boy from County Donegal in Ulster, is said to have stolen passage on a ship to America around 1765. He settled at Carlisle in Cumberland County, and there, in 1772, he and Mary married and made powder together.

With two small children and a British invasion of Pennsylvania threatening, the Pattons sold their Carlisle mill and moved south and west into the Overmountain country, then part of North Carolina and now East Tennessee. With help from Andrew Taylor they built a new mill on a creek that came to be called Powder Branch.

In September 1780 the militia of the Overmountain settlements gathered at Sycamore Shoals, close to the Patton mill, before crossing the mountains to meet a Loyalist army. Mary's powder, at least 500 pounds of it, went with them to the American victory at Kings Mountain on 7 October 1780.

![The Overmountain Men gathering at Sycamore Shoals](media/stories/overmountain-gathering.jpg "*The Gathering of the Overmountain Men at Sycamore Shoals*, painted by Lloyd Branson in 1915. More than a thousand militiamen, with their families seeing them off, met there on 25 September 1780.")

The march took them across the Blue Ridge and down to Kings Mountain, on the border between the Carolinas. There they surrounded Major Patrick Ferguson's Loyalist militia on the ridge top. The fight lasted about an hour. Ferguson was killed, and nearly all of his men were killed, wounded or captured. Thomas Jefferson later called the victory "the turn of the tide of success" in the war.

![The Battle of Kings Mountain](media/stories/kings-mountain-chappel.jpg "The Battle of King's Mountain, an engraving after a painting by Alonzo Chappel. The riflemen left their horses at the foot of the ridge and climbed toward Ferguson's lines.")

> Stories handed down in the family say Mary shod her own mare for powder deliveries, once bluffed a masked highwayman by telling him her husband was just behind with the money, and after the war rode as far as South Carolina to sell powder at about a dollar a pound.

John died at Milligan in 1808. Mary died on her 85th birthday, 15 December 1836, and is buried in the Patton-Simmons Cemetery on Powder Branch, where her descendants put up a monument in 1932. One of her iron kettles is in the Rocky Mount museum.

![Rocky Mount, Tennessee](media/stories/rocky-mount-1910.jpg "Rocky Mount in winter, a print by Allen Ross Pittman. The log house in Sullivan County served as the first capital of the territory south of the Ohio River from 1790 to 1792, and is now a museum.")

Their daughter [[Margaret Patton (1773)|Margaret "Peggy" Patton]] married [[Brewer McKeehan (1770)|Brewer McKeehan]], and the McKeehans of this tree descend from that couple twice over. That story is told in *Two paths to Brewer and Peggy*.
